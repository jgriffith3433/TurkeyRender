# Story and script craft, against what we did

Written 2026-11-08 after J: "Don't you understand the flow of a video? There's a way to tell stories with timing, subjects, props, zoom, pauses, and dialogue and suspense etc." Sources at the end. Most are secondary summaries of the classic texts (McKee's *Story*, *Hitchcock/Truffaut*, Every Frame a Painting's Edgar Wright essay). Treat them as working rules, not law.

## The rules

| Craft rule | What it means | What Hawkes Punch v1 did |
|---|---|---|
| **A scene turns.** | Every scene changes something that matters to the main character, from + to −, or − to +. If nothing turns, it is "activity without movement": cut it or rewrite it. | Space was activity without movement: stunt after stunt, nothing changed for him. |
| **A desire, an obstacle, a turn.** | Each scene: what he wants, what blocks it, how it ends differently. | He wanted nothing in space. The double had no reason to be there. |
| **A short is hook, conflict, payoff.** | 3-5 core beats. Escalate in 2-3 steps. The payoff lands in the last seconds. | 30+ beats in space, no escalation curve, and an ending with no payoff. |
| **Setups and payoffs.** | Every prop, visual and line that matters is planted, then paid off. Check both ways: no payoff without a setup, no setup without a payoff. | The space ghost was set up and never paid off. The double was paid off (the ad crawl) and never set up. The can never mattered. |
| **Suspense beats surprise.** | Show the audience the bomb under the table (more knowledge than the character), then make them wait. Add a clock. | All surprise, no suspense: the punch came from nowhere. Nothing told us she could punch. |
| **The rule of three.** | Two similar beats set a pattern, the third breaks it. | No patterns, so no breaks. |
| **The pause before the punchline.** | Hold before the joke; too long loses them. In film, the pause is a held face or a beat of silence, built in the edit. | Cuts landed on a 2.5 s clock, not on the joke. |
| **The reaction is the punchline.** | Cut to the face that reacts: a close-up or a medium close-up. No filler sound under it. | Actions had no reactions, and reactions had no actions. |
| **Physical comedy lives in the wide.** | Show the whole body and the cause and effect in one frame. | Space throws were cut across shots that didn't line up. |
| **Shot size follows intensity.** | Establish wide; tighten into bigger close-ups as a scene builds. Don't shoot everything mid or long. | Random sizes; no establishing shot for space; the scale changed every cut. |
| **Visual comedy (Edgar Wright).** | Gags made by framing and what's out of frame: things entering the frame, precisely timed sound effects, match cuts and parallel shots, repetition that pays off. | The off-screen fist button was right. The rest wasn't designed this way. |
| **Clear geography.** | The audience must know where everyone is: one establishing shot, consistent screen sides, consistent scale. | Broken throughout space. |

## How we work from now on
1. **Write a real script first**, in screenplay form: scene heading, action, dialogue, (parenthetical), with each scene's desire, obstacle and turn named above it.
2. **Keep a setup/payoff ledger.** Every plant has its payoff and every payoff has its plant. Check it before staging.
3. **Plan suspense.** Where can the audience know more than him? Show it, then make them wait.
4. **Plan each joke:** setup, pause, punchline, reaction. Name the shot each part is in and its size.
5. **Shot plan from the script,** not cards first: an establishing shot per place, sizes that tighten as a scene builds, camera moves only when motivated (a push-in on a realisation).
6. **A cold-viewer test on the animatic or previs** before polish: a reviewer who never read the script retells the story. Every gap between that retelling and the script gets a fix.
